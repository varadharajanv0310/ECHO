ECHO

A social network where what you say belongs to a place instead of to you, travels only when somebody chooses to carry it, and dies when nobody does.

Open the sky Source
01 — The idea

A feed is a shape, and every rule follows from it

Almost every complaint about social media is a complaint about one structural decision: content is attached to an author and delivered down a ranked, endless column. Once that shape exists, everything else is forced. If posts are attached to people, people need follower counts. If there is a column, something has to order it. If something orders it, engagement becomes the currency. If engagement is the currency, the loudest thing wins, and nothing is ever allowed to end.

ECHO does not add rules on top of that shape to make it kinder. It removes the shape. Content is attached to a place, and the places are laid out in space you have to physically travel through. There is no column to rank because there is no column. There are no follower counts because nothing is addressed to followers. Nothing here is a policy — it is what is left when the feed is gone.

02 — What is gone

Removed, and why it could not come back

Each of these is missing because the architecture gives it nowhere to live, not because a setting is switched off.

  • The feed There is no column of posts. Signals sit in the place they were sent to, and you see them by going there. Nothing arrives at you.
  • Ranking Nothing is sorted by predicted engagement. Search results are deliberately scrambled — there is no best result in a place with no scores, so there is nothing to put at the top.
  • Followers You cannot subscribe to a person's output, because output is not attached to people. You can add somebody, which opens a conversation. It does not create a channel.
  • Likes and counts No number goes up. The only visible metric is how many people are carrying something, and that is not a score — it is the reason it is still alive.
  • Permanence Every signal is given hours, not forever. There is no archive, no recovery, and no notification when something of yours has gone.
  • Infinite scroll You navigate by flying into a place and back out of it. Every view has edges, and every session has an obvious end.
03 — What replaces it

Three rules, and everything else falls out of them

  • Belonging A signal is addressed to a place, never to an audience. You choose where it lands, and anyone who walks into that place can find it. Your own star is where your signals happen to orbit — not who they are for.
  • Carrying Opening somebody's signal lets you carry it. It starts orbiting you as well, keeping their name and their colour, and its clock starts again. This is the only way anything travels, and the only way anything survives.
  • Decay Silence is fatal, by default. A signal nobody carries fades from violet to amber and disappears. Attention is not a score here; it is life support.
04 — The sky

Why it is a galaxy, and not a metaphor

The space theme is not a skin over a list. The sky is the data model, drawn at the scale it actually has — and every visual property in it is carrying information that a feed would have had to state in words.

05 — Walkthrough

Using it, from the first frame

The whole thing runs in your browser. There is no account, no email, no password and no server — your profile and your signals are in this browser's local storage and nowhere else. Clearing site data is a complete, irreversible delete.

Arrive

A loading frame, then the wordmark, then a long road that you scroll down. Keep scrolling: the road resolves into a galaxy hanging in front of you. Sound is on the small player in the top right — it is worth it.

Click the galaxy

It ignites, and you are asked who you are: a name that is not a username and is not unique, one line about why you are listening, a mark, a colour, up to three traits, and the places you want to be in. Then you dive through it and come out inside the sky.

Take the tour

Six cards on what this place is, then a pass along the left rail with each control lit in turn. It runs once. You can start it again any time from Menu → Settings → Tutorial.

Fly into a place

Drag to look around. Click one of the six names to fly into it, and its figure draws itself between the people standing there. The trail at the top of the screen — CLUSTER / 3AM / HAVEL — is also how you get back out.

Stand at somebody

Click a person and the camera drops into their system: their star at the centre, their signals on rings around it, each one labelled with what it actually says. Click the same person again to open who they are.

Open something, and carry it

Click a planet and it opens in the bar at the bottom, with how many people are carrying it and how long it has left. Carry this puts it into your own sky as well and restarts its clock. That is the one action in ECHO that changes anything for somebody else.

Send something of your own

The + on the rail. Choose the place it lands in, write it, and give it 12 hours, a day, or three days. It appears immediately, orbiting your own star, with the clock already running.

06 — The rail

Profile

Your name, one line, mark, banner and colour, plus shelves for games and music picked from a catalogue, and handles for anywhere else you are. The colour is the important one: it tints your star in the sky, your cursor, and every glass surface in your interface. Visiting somebody re-tints the whole window to their colour, so a profile feels like walking into a room rather than reading a record.

Menu

Light or dark ground, four accents, film grain, reduced flashing, and what reaches you. Light mode is not an inversion — it is handled inside the shaders, so glow becomes pigment and the sky turns to ink on paper. The opening sequence and the tutorial can both be replayed from here.

Dashboard

Split three ways on purpose, because most platforms collapse these into one stream of notifications and flatten the difference between a person replying to you and a number moving. Sent is your signals and how long each has left. Responses is what came back. Messages is the people you are actually talking to.

07 — Construction

How it is built

Entirely front-end. No backend, no database, no analytics, no third-party calls at runtime. Everything below ships in the bundle.

Rendering

React 19 and TypeScript on Vite, with three.js through React Three Fiber for every spatial scene.

Shaders

Hand-written GLSL: a domain-warped nebula, the perspective-divided road, the galaxy, and the sky — one draw call for every place, person and signal at once.

The sky

Generated from a seeded PRNG, so it is identical on every visit and on every machine. Nothing about it is stored.

Sound

Synthesised live in the Web Audio API — detuned oscillators, a moving filter, a brown-noise bed. No audio files.

Type

Archivo Variable, animated along its width axis, with Geist Mono for anything the machine says. Both self-hosted.

Storage

localStorage, and nothing else. Your profile, signals, carries and messages never leave the browser.

What is simulated. ECHO has no server, so there is nobody else really online. The other people in the sky are generated, and what they do with your signals is derived from a hash of the signal's id and the time — the same on every render and across a reload, with nothing written down. Roughly a quarter of signals are never carried by anyone, because a reply has to be able to not arrive. It is an honest model of the mechanic, not a mock-up of one.

08 — Scope

What this is not

It is a working argument, not a product with users. There is no real network between browsers, no moderation model, and no answer yet for what any of this looks like on a phone — the layout is built for a desktop screen, and a small-screen version would need to be art-directed rather than reflowed.

The point it is making is narrower than a social network and, I think, more useful: that most of what we dislike about these places is downstream of one shape, and that changing the shape changes the behaviour without anyone having to be told to behave.
